Australia's Dirty Dozen would look different. We just haven't checked since 2014.

Every year a US list ranks fruit and veg by pesticide traces. Here is what Australia's own tests found, how much of each chemical people actually ate, and how old that check is.

Scroll to start

This is the 2026 Dirty Dozen. The Environmental Working Group, a US advocacy group, ranks produce by the pesticide traces found in US government tests. Spinach came first this year.

Australian shoppers see the list too. But every number behind it comes from produce sold in the US.

Australia does test its own food. Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) runs the Australian Total Diet Study. It buys ordinary groceries around the country and sends them to a lab.

Its last pesticide round covered 88 foods. Only 5 of the Dirty Dozen were in it as fresh produce.

Spinach, kale, cherries, blackberries, pears and blueberries were not tested at all. Peaches were only tested canned, and nothing turned up.

So the top of the US list is mostly foods Australia's diet study never looked at.

So what would an Australian list look like? These are all 25 fresh fruits and vegetables in the study.

They are ranked the way FSANZ reported them: by how many different farm chemicals turned up across each food's samples.

Bok choi tops it, with 16 different chemicals across 8 samples. Capsicum and cucumber are near the top too.

None of those three are on the US list.

Apples, strawberries, grapes and nectarines are high on both lists.

Potatoes are 11th in the US. Here, only 2 chemicals turned up, each in a single sample.

Two of the US "Clean Fifteen" look less clean here. Every cauliflower sample had dithiocarbamate fungicides. Carbendazim, another fungicide, was in 6 of 8 mushroom samples.

Five had nothing at all: avocados, garlic, kiwifruit, onions and pumpkin.

A count of chemicals says nothing about the amounts, though. That is the part that matters for health.

Each dot is one chemical the study found. Further right means people ate more of it.

The scale is the acceptable daily intake: the amount you could eat every day for life without appreciable risk to health. The line marks 100% of it.

These dots are a worst case. For each chemical we show the age group that ate the most, and the top 10% of eaters in that group.

Even so, 49 of the 52 chemicals FSANZ assessed stay under 10% of the safe amount. Most are under 1%.

Two get closer. Dithiocarbamate fungicides reach 80% for heavy-eating 2 to 5 year olds. Broccoli, grapes and cauliflower had the most.

Propargite, a mite killer found on nectarines and apples, reaches 50%.

One went over. Prothiofos, an insecticide, came only from grapes.

For 2 to 5 year olds who ate grapes, the average dose was double the safe amount. The top 10% got four times it.

Every one of those grape samples was legal. The residues were well under the maximum residue limit, the legal cap for grapes.

The legal limit and the safe limit had come apart. After FSANZ raised it, the company holding the approval cancelled its use on grapes.

That check is old. The groceries were bought in May 2013 and February 2014. The report came out in June 2019.

FSANZ has published two total diet studies since. One tested dioxins and the other PFAS. Neither tested pesticides.

Twelve years after the shopping trip, it is still the latest look at pesticides across the Australian diet.

Newer testing exists, but it is narrow. The National Residue Survey checks apples and pears every year for the fruit industry. It only reports samples near or over the legal limit.

Each square here is one apple sample from 2024-25.

In 2024-25, 6 of 170 apple samples were over the legal limit for the fungicide iprodione. Another 4 were above half of it.

The year before, it was 2 of 166 over.

So is there an Australian Dirty Dozen?

Not one you can build honestly from public data. The only full check says most traces were tiny, with one real problem caught and fixed. But it tested food from 2014, and nobody has published a repeat.

What it can't tell you is whether today's bok choi or grapes look the same. A new pesticide round of the Total Diet Study would answer that.

    How this was made

    The Australian results are from the 25th Australian Total Diet Study (FSANZ, June 2019): Table A3.9 for which chemicals were found in which foods, and Appendix 11 for dietary exposure as a share of the acceptable daily intake. FSANZ bought 1,524 grocery items and blended them in threes into 508 composite samples. Most fresh produce had 8 composites each (mango and garlic had 4). 172 of the 508 had at least one farm chemical.

    A composite blends three purchases, so "found in 2 of 8 samples" means at least 2 of 24 purchases. These numbers can't be compared with US rates, which come from single samples.

    The ranking counts different chemicals per food, as FSANZ did. It ignores amounts. The dots use the highest 90th percentile exposure across age groups, for people who ate the food. Chemicals under 1% are drawn at 0.5%.

    Apple results are from the National Residue Survey annual datasets for 2023-24 and 2024-25 (Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry). The survey is run for industry and only reports results above half the legal limit.

    The 2026 Dirty Dozen and Clean Fifteen are from the Environmental Working Group, as reported by Food Safety News on 8 April 2026.
